# Incident 2026-05-06 — production database branch auto-deleted

Production sign-in started failing on May 1, six days after I created the
prod Neon branch. I noticed mid-session while finishing the SDK publish
work for Task 8.1, ruled out four wrong hypotheses across two days, and
recovered on May 6 once I figured out the actual cause: Neon's free-tier
branch creation flow has a 1/3/7-day expiration option, and I'd picked
the 7-day version on April 29 without reading the field. The branch
expired on schedule. Recovery itself took ~15 minutes.

## Timeline

- **Apr 29** — Created prod Neon branch as part of Task 2.4 (Vercel
  deploy). Selected a non-permanent expiration during creation. Didn't
  notice or document the choice.
- **Apr 30 – May 5** — Working Weeks 6–7 (hero screen) and Task 8.1 (SDK
  publish). All work targeted dev. Prod went unvisited; the auth flow
  there was never exercised.
- **May 1 ~13:30 UTC** — Visited tracebee.dev mid-session, clicked Sign
  in with GitHub, got a 500. Pulled Vercel logs.
- **May 1 – May 5** — Wrong-hypothesis triage: cold-start Neon compute,
  `DATABASE_URL` rotation, schema drift, manual deletion. Each ruled out
  but none replaced.
- **May 6 ~14:00 UTC** — Opened Neon dashboard, found the prod branch
  was gone (not suspended — gone). Recreated, updated Vercel env,
  redeployed, verified sign-in. Found the expiration UI on the second
  branch creation: a small radio in the create-branch form, default
  selected to a non-permanent option on free tier.

Total elapsed wall-clock: 5 days. Total time actively investigating:
~90 minutes across two sessions. Most of the gap was me not noticing,
because production was being routed around — not because the failure
was hard to see.

## What broke (technical)

Auth.js v5 wraps every database call through its adapter. When the
adapter throws, it logs `AdapterError` and the behavior depends on the
caller:

- **Session lookup on the homepage** (`auth()` in the layout) — Auth.js
  catches the error internally and returns `null`. The page renders as
  if the user isn't signed in. The error appears in the logs but the
  user sees a working homepage.
- **OAuth callback** (`/api/auth/callback/github`) — has to write
  rows to `users`, `accounts`, and `sessions`. There's no
  graceful-degradation path; if the writes fail, Auth.js redirects to
  `/api/auth/error`, which itself crashes (probably because the error
  page also tries to read from the DB). Result: 500.

This asymmetry — homepage masks, sign-in surfaces — is the part worth
recording. I assumed "broken DB → broken site." The real shape was
"broken DB → broken sign-in, working homepage," which made detection
slower and made my first hypothesis (cold-start timing) look more
plausible than it was.

## Why detection took five days

Three compounding reasons:

1. **Homepage masking.** The site looked fine. If sign-in had been on
   the landing page, I'd have caught this on the first prod visit.
2. **My own dev/prod separation worked too well.** Weeks 6–7 was UI
   work against the dev DB; 8.1 was SDK packaging against npm. Neither
   touched prod. The discipline I'd been congratulating myself for
   ("never accidentally touch prod") meant I also never accidentally
   *probed* prod.
3. **No health check.** No cron, no synthetic monitor, no "is prod
   alive" anything. The first signal was a manual visit.

The thing that's actually load-bearing here is #3. Masking and
separation are facts of the environment; (1) and (2) compound but
aren't the root failure of *detection*. A daily `SELECT 1` against
prod with email-on-failure would have collapsed five days into one.

## What I ruled out, in order

Each was wrong, but each was the right next thing to check:

- **Vercel deploy broken.** Homepage rendered → no.
- **`DATABASE_URL` missing or rotated.** Module would have crashed at
  init → no.
- **Schema drift.** Couldn't verify without working DB access at the
  time of triage. Eventually irrelevant — the DB didn't exist to drift.
- **Neon compute cold-start.** Plausible because Auth.js chains
  several queries on the OAuth callback and a slow first wake could
  exceed the request timeout. Ruled out by checking the Neon dashboard
  and seeing no compute at all, suspended or otherwise.
- **Manual deletion (I deleted it accidentally).** Reasonable
  hypothesis — I have a documented history of dev/prod tooling
  confusion (Weeks 3–4 mishap). I didn't, but ruling it out required
  forensics.
- **Audit log forensics.** Wanted to read the Neon Operations / Audit
  Log to see who-deleted-what-when. Free tier doesn't include one.
  Closed avenue.

Only after all of those did I look at *the create-branch form* with
fresh eyes and spot the expiration option. The lesson there is the
specific bug I keep making: assuming defaults are "permanent" or
"safe" without reading the form. Same shape as the
`crypto.randomUUID()` bug from Week 2 — wrong assumption about a
silent default, manifests later as a confusing failure.

## Root cause

Neon's free-tier branch creation has an "expiration" option (a small
radio group in the create-branch dialog) with 1, 3, and 7-day choices
and, depending on plan, a "Never" option that may or may not be
available. I picked 7 days on Apr 29 without registering it as a
choice — the way you click through a dialog when the defaults look
fine.

The Week 2 decisions doc documented every other branch-creation
decision (Schema Only vs. Latest Data, branches over separate
projects, the `__drizzle_migrations` recovery) but not the expiration
field. That's the real failure: a silent assumption ("branches are
permanent until I delete them") rendered into config and never
written down. When the assumption turned out to be wrong, there was
nothing to consult.

The branch expired on schedule, exactly 7 days later. No warning
email, no dashboard banner, no audit trail — just gone.

## Recovery

Six steps, ~15 minutes once I knew what to do:

1. Created a new prod branch in Neon, branched from dev with "Latest
   data" — copies the schema and the populated `__drizzle_migrations`
   table so migrations don't try to re-run. Set expiration to **never**
   this time.
2. Copied the new pooled connection string.
3. Updated `DATABASE_URL` in Vercel for the **Production** environment
   only. Left Preview and Development alone.
4. Pushed an empty commit to trigger a redeploy. Vercel doesn't
   re-deploy on env-var changes alone.
5. Verified by signing into tracebee.dev. Sign-in worked, dashboard
   loaded. Adapter healthy.
6. Old API key (issued against the deleted branch) is dead, since the
   `api_keys` row is gone. Nothing to revoke; the key just doesn't
   match anymore. Will reissue when next sending traces.

## Lessons + action items

Pull forward from Week 9, in priority order:

1. **Daily prod-DB health check** (Week 9). Concretely: a cron job that
   does `GET https://www.tracebee.dev/api/health` (a route that does a
   `SELECT 1` against the DB and returns 200/503), with email-on-failure.
   Vercel Cron + a tiny route handler. ~30 min of work. This is the
   biggest detection win and should be done this week, not in June.

2. **Backfill the Week 2 decisions doc** with a note about the Neon
   expiration option. The lesson dies with this file otherwise. Anyone
   reading the Week 2 doc to understand "how was prod set up" should see
   the expiration-was-a-trap note. ~5 min.

3. **Reconsider the Neon plan.** Free tier with 7-day branch expiration
   is a structural problem for a project that wants to keep prod alive.
   Either upgrade (~$19/mo for the lowest paid tier last I checked,
   gives permanent branches and audit logs) or accept that prod needs a
   weekly "extend the branch" task on the calendar. Neither is wrong;
   the call is yours. For a portfolio project shipping in July, paid for
   two months is ~$40 — small relative to the time cost of another
   incident.

4. **The migration-script wrapper already on Week 9 wouldn't have
   helped here.** Different failure mode (Neon-side auto-deletion, not
   user-side accidental deletion). Worth recording so the next incident
   doesn't get used to over-promote that backlog item by association.

## What surprised me

The homepage masking. I'd internalized "broken DB = visible failure"
without examining it, and Auth.js's tolerance was the missing piece.
For an observability tool whose whole pitch is "see what's actually
happening," catching a five-day production outage by manual page visit
is a particular kind of irony.

That a free-tier policy can quietly delete a database with no warning
email. I'd assumed "free tier" meant "fewer features" but not "data
loss without notice." That's a category of provider behavior I
hadn't priced in for an MVP project.

The 7-day timing fitting so exactly. Once I saw "Apr 29 + 7 = May 6,"
the entire mystery collapsed into one obvious answer. Hours of "is
this a network thing? a cold-start thing? a permission thing?"
evaporated. Worth recording because that's the shape of these
incidents in general — confusing until you find the one fact that
makes the rest obvious, then trivial.
